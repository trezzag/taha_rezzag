What is a Rotating Detonation Engine, and Why Does it Matter?

Imagine a combustion wave spiraling around the inside of an annular chamber at nearly 2,000 meters per second. That's not a malfunction — that's the entire point.

For most of the history of air-breathing propulsion, engineers have relied on deflagration — subsonic, pressure-dropping combustion that we call burning. It works. It has powered everything from the Wright Flyer to modern turbofans. But it carries a fundamental thermodynamic cost: combustion in a conventional engine happens at approximately constant pressure, and you leave efficiency on the table.

Detonation combustion is different. A detonation wave is a coupled shock-reaction front that propagates supersonically, consuming fuel and releasing energy faster than the surrounding gas can respond. Crucially, it burns at nearly constant volume — which, by the laws of thermodynamics, is more efficient. The Humphrey cycle (detonation-based) extracts more work from a given amount of fuel than the Brayton cycle (deflagration-based) that conventional gas turbines operate on.

The Rotating Architecture

Pulse detonation engines attempted to exploit this advantage in the 1990s and 2000s, using repeated detonation pulses in a tube. The concept worked in principle but suffered from fill-purge inefficiencies and mechanical complexity. The rotating detonation engine takes a different approach: instead of repeated pulses, it establishes one or more detonation waves that continuously orbit an annular combustion chamber.

Fresh propellant is injected from one end. The traveling detonation wave consumes it. Combustion products exit from the other end. The result is a nearly continuous process, with far less mechanical complexity than pulsed systems. The wave self-sustains as long as fuel and oxidizer are supplied — it's a kind of perpetual detonative motion, governed entirely by chemistry and fluid mechanics.

Why Now?

Several converging factors have made RDE research tractable and urgent. High-frequency pressure transducers can now survive the brutal acoustic environment inside an RDE long enough to produce useful data. Computational fluid dynamics codes have matured to the point where detonation wave interactions can be meaningfully simulated. And the aerospace industry is hungry for efficiency gains — in an era of sustainable aviation and contested space access, even a few percentage points of specific impulse improvement matter enormously.

The challenges remain significant. Wave stability, heat loads, injector robustness, and integration with turbomachinery are all active research problems. But the physics are real, and the potential is substantial. That's what brings me to the lab every day.

May 2025 Thermodynamics

The Humphrey Cycle: Why Detonation Beats Deflagration

A thermodynamic comparison of the Brayton and Humphrey cycles — and what the math tells us about the upper limits of pressure-gain combustion efficiency.

The efficiency advantage of detonation over deflagration isn't hand-waving — it falls directly out of classical thermodynamics. Let's work through it.

The Brayton Cycle

Conventional gas turbines operate on the Brayton cycle: isentropic compression, constant-pressure heat addition (combustion), and isentropic expansion. The constant-pressure combustion step is the key constraint. In reality, the combustor of a gas turbine is designed to maintain near-constant total pressure, meaning the thermodynamic process doesn't exploit the pressure rise that would theoretically be available from more intense heat release.

The Humphrey Cycle

The Humphrey cycle replaces constant-pressure combustion with constant-volume combustion — a better approximation of what happens in a detonation event. Because the gas cannot expand during heat addition, all the energy goes into raising the temperature and pressure simultaneously. The subsequent expansion from a higher pressure state yields more work.

The thermal efficiency advantage depends on the heat release rate and the specific heat ratio of the gas, but analyses consistently show efficiency gains of 10–25% depending on conditions. For a rocket engine or gas turbine where fuel cost is a primary constraint, that's a transformative improvement.

The Catch

The Humphrey cycle is an idealization. Real detonation events don't happen at perfectly constant volume, and the wave-based combustion in an RDE involves a complex mix of processes at different stations. Some of the theoretical efficiency advantage is recovered; not all of it. Ongoing research — including work happening at UCF — is aimed at understanding exactly how much, and under what conditions.

April 2025 Diagnostics

Chasing the Wave: High-Speed Diagnostics in RDE Research

How researchers use pressure transducers, chemiluminescence imaging, and schlieren photography to study combustion events that last microseconds.

Measuring something that travels at 1,500–2,000 m/s inside a metal tube, at temperatures exceeding 2,500 K, while withstanding pressures that spike and recover in microseconds — this is the diagnostic challenge at the heart of RDE research.

Pressure Transducers

High-frequency PCB piezoelectric pressure transducers are the workhorses of RDE diagnostics. Mounted flush with the combustor wall at multiple axial and circumferential locations, they capture the pressure signature of the detonation wave as it passes. From the time-of-flight between adjacent sensors, we can compute wave speed. From the peak-to-trough pressure ratio, we can infer the strength of the wave and assess whether true detonation or a weaker deflagration wave is present.

Chemiluminescence Imaging

When fuel burns, excited radical species (OH*, CH*, C₂*) emit light at characteristic wavelengths. OH* emission at 310 nm is particularly useful — it marks the reaction zone of the flame front with high spatial resolution. By imaging through a transparent endwall with a high-speed camera (often 100,000+ fps) fitted with an appropriate bandpass filter, we can directly visualize the rotating detonation wave and count the number of waves present in the annulus.

Schlieren Photography

Schlieren imaging exploits the fact that density gradients in a gas bend light. Shock waves, which involve abrupt density jumps, show up clearly as bright or dark lines in a schlieren image. Applied to an optically accessible RDE section, schlieren photography can reveal the shock structure associated with the detonation wave — including the triple point, the reflected shocks, and the contact surfaces between fresh and burned gas.

Getting these diagnostics to work simultaneously, time-synchronized to microsecond precision, in an environment that vibrates, heats up rapidly, and sometimes tries to destroy your sensor suite — that's where much of the real experimental engineering happens.

March 2025 Theory

Wave Stability and the Limits of Detonative Operation

Not all RDE operation is created equal. Understanding the boundaries between stable detonation, unstable multi-wave modes, and deflagration is critical for engine design.

One of the more counterintuitive aspects of RDE operation is that having more detonation waves isn't always better — and sometimes fewer waves, or even one, is the design target. The wave count is a function of the chamber geometry, propellant injection characteristics, and total mass flow rate, and understanding how to control it is an open research problem.

Single vs. Multi-Wave Modes

RDEs can operate with one, two, three, or more detonation waves traveling simultaneously around the annulus. Each wave configuration has different propagation speeds, pressure gains, and stability characteristics. Some operating conditions produce chaotic multi-wave interactions where waves merge and split unpredictably — a mode that's fascinating from a physics standpoint but problematic for an engine.

The Deflagration-to-Detonation Transition

RDEs don't always start in detonation mode — they typically ignite in deflagration and must transition. How reliably and quickly that transition occurs depends on the injector design, the local equivalence ratio, and the geometry of the chamber. Research into the deflagration-to-detonation transition (DDT) in annular geometries is an active area, with implications for engine start reliability and operability range.

Extinction Limits

Like any combustion process, rotating detonation has lean and rich extinction limits — conditions outside of which the wave cannot sustain itself. Mapping these limits for different propellant combinations and injection pressures is essential for defining the operational envelope of a practical RDE.

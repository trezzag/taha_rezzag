Igniting the

I'm a propulsion researcher at the University of Central Florida, where my work sits at the intersection of combustion physics, thermodynamics, and aerospace engineering. My primary focus is rotating detonation engines (RDEs) — a class of pressure-gain combustion devices with the potential to dramatically improve propulsive efficiency.

RDEs exploit the thermodynamic advantages of detonation combustion, operating on a wave-based cycle that traverses an annular combustion chamber at supersonic speeds. The physics are extreme and the engineering challenges are profound — which is exactly what drew me here.

What is a Rotating Detonation Engine, and Why Does it Matter?

Imagine a combustion wave spiraling around the inside of an annular chamber at nearly 2,000 meters per second. That's not a malfunction — that's the entire point.

For most of the history of air-breathing propulsion, engineers have relied on deflagration — subsonic, pressure-dropping combustion that we simply call burning. It works. It has powered everything from the Wright Flyer to modern turbofans. But it carries a fundamental thermodynamic cost: combustion in a conventional engine happens at approximately constant pressure, and you leave efficiency on the table.

Detonation combustion is different. A detonation wave is a coupled shock-reaction front that propagates supersonically, consuming fuel and releasing energy faster than the surrounding gas can respond. Crucially, it burns at nearly constant volume — which, by the laws of thermodynamics, is more efficient. The Humphrey cycle (detonation-based) extracts more work from a given amount of fuel than the Brayton cycle (deflagration-based) that conventional gas turbines operate on.

The Rotating Architecture

Pulse detonation engines attempted to exploit this advantage in the 1990s and 2000s, using repeated detonation pulses in a tube. The concept worked in principle but suffered from fill-purge inefficiencies and mechanical complexity. The rotating detonation engine takes a different approach: instead of repeated pulses, it establishes one or more detonation waves that continuously orbit an annular combustion chamber.

Fresh propellant is injected from one end. The traveling detonation wave consumes it. Combustion products exit from the other end. The result is a nearly continuous process — the wave self-sustains as long as fuel and oxidizer are supplied, governed entirely by chemistry and fluid mechanics.

Why Now?

Several converging factors have made RDE research tractable and urgent. High-frequency pressure transducers can now survive the brutal acoustic environment inside an RDE long enough to produce useful data. CFD codes have matured to the point where detonation wave interactions can be meaningfully simulated. And the aerospace industry is hungry for efficiency gains — in an era of sustainable aviation and contested space access, even a few percentage points of specific impulse improvement matter enormously.

The Humphrey Cycle: Why Detonation Beats Deflagration

A thermodynamic comparison of the Brayton and Humphrey cycles — and what the math tells us about the upper limits of pressure-gain combustion efficiency.

The efficiency advantage of detonation over deflagration isn't hand-waving — it falls directly out of classical thermodynamics.

The Brayton Cycle

Conventional gas turbines operate on the Brayton cycle: isentropic compression, constant-pressure heat addition (combustion), and isentropic expansion. The constant-pressure combustion step is the key constraint — it doesn't exploit the pressure rise that would theoretically be available from more intense heat release.

The Humphrey Cycle

The Humphrey cycle replaces constant-pressure combustion with constant-volume combustion — a better approximation of what happens in a detonation event. Because the gas cannot expand during heat addition, all the energy goes into raising the temperature and pressure simultaneously. The subsequent expansion from a higher pressure state yields more work. Analyses consistently show efficiency gains of 10–25% depending on conditions.

The Catch

The Humphrey cycle is an idealization. Real detonation events don't happen at perfectly constant volume, and the wave-based combustion in an RDE involves a complex mix of processes. Some of the theoretical efficiency advantage is recovered; not all of it. Ongoing research is aimed at understanding exactly how much, and under what conditions.

Chasing the Wave: High-Speed Diagnostics in RDE Research

How researchers use pressure transducers, chemiluminescence imaging, and schlieren photography to study combustion events that last microseconds.

Measuring something that travels at 1,500–2,000 m/s inside a metal tube, at temperatures exceeding 2,500 K, while withstanding pressures that spike and recover in microseconds — this is the diagnostic challenge at the heart of RDE research.

Pressure Transducers

High-frequency PCB piezoelectric pressure transducers are the workhorses of RDE diagnostics. Mounted flush with the combustor wall, they capture the pressure signature of the detonation wave as it passes. From the time-of-flight between adjacent sensors, we can compute wave speed. From the peak-to-trough pressure ratio, we can infer whether true detonation or a weaker deflagration wave is present.

Chemiluminescence Imaging

When fuel burns, excited radical species (OH*, CH*, C₂*) emit light at characteristic wavelengths. OH* emission at 310 nm marks the reaction zone with high spatial resolution. By imaging through a transparent endwall with a high-speed camera (often 100,000+ fps), we can directly visualize the rotating detonation wave and count the number of waves in the annulus.

Schlieren Photography

Schlieren imaging exploits density gradients in a gas to reveal shock structure. Applied to an optically accessible RDE section, it can reveal the triple point, reflected shocks, and contact surfaces between fresh and burned gas. Getting these diagnostics time-synchronized to microsecond precision in a brutal thermal environment — that's where much of the real experimental engineering happens.

Wave Stability and the Limits of Detonative Operation

Not all RDE operation is created equal. Understanding the boundaries between stable detonation, unstable multi-wave modes, and deflagration is critical for engine design.

One of the more counterintuitive aspects of RDE operation is that having more detonation waves isn't always better. The wave count is a function of chamber geometry, propellant injection characteristics, and total mass flow rate — and understanding how to control it is an open research problem.

Single vs. Multi-Wave Modes

RDEs can operate with one, two, three, or more detonation waves traveling simultaneously around the annulus. Each wave configuration has different propagation speeds, pressure gains, and stability characteristics. Some operating conditions produce chaotic multi-wave interactions where waves merge and split unpredictably — fascinating from a physics standpoint, but problematic for an engine.

The DDT Problem

RDEs don't always start in detonation mode — they typically ignite in deflagration and must transition. How reliably and quickly the deflagration-to-detonation transition (DDT) occurs depends on the injector design, local equivalence ratio, and chamber geometry. Research into DDT in annular geometries is an active area with direct implications for engine start reliability.

Extinction Limits

Like any combustion process, rotating detonation has lean and rich extinction limits. Mapping these limits for different propellant combinations and injection pressures is essential for defining the operational envelope of a practical RDE.
